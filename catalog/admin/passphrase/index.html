Capitalising is a fixed rule an attacker can replay, so it adds no strength; it is only there for sites that demand a capital letter. Extra digits do add strength (3.32 bits each).

What does the bits number mean?

Entropy in bits describes the generator, not the string. It is the number of coin flips it would take to reproduce this exact choice: n bits means 2ⁿ equally likely outcomes, and every extra bit doubles the attacker's work.

The number here is computed from what actually happened: length × log2(alphabet size) for passwords, and words × log2(wordlist size) for passphrases, using the real sizes after your exclusions. It assumes the attacker knows everything except the random draws: your settings, this exact wordlist, the separator. That is the honest assumption; secrecy of the method is not strength.

A string that merely looks random but was picked by a human has far less entropy than its length suggests. That is why this number cannot be measured from the characters; only the generator knows it.

Crack times below are averages (half the keyspace) at two speeds: 1,000 guesses/sec for an attack against a live login, and 100 billion/sec for an offline attack on a stolen database hashed with something fast. A slow hash such as bcrypt or Argon2 is far harder than the offline figure; a leaked plaintext database is instant regardless. Aim for 75+ bits for anything you care about.

Nothing is stored or transmitted. This page has no network code of any kind: no scripts, fonts or images are fetched, and no password is ever sent, logged or saved. Pull the plug on your connection and it still works. Generated secrets live only in this tab until you close or regenerate.

Your settings (length, options) are kept in this browser's local storage. Passwords never are.

After copying, your clipboard holds the secret until you copy something else.
